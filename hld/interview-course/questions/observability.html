Design Observability

Interviewers use this to check whether you can operate what you build: turning logs, metrics, and traces into detection and diagnosis, and paging a human only when action is genuinely needed.

One-line answerInstrument every service with structured logs, low-cardinality metrics, and sampled traces that share a trace ID, then page on SLO burn for user-visible symptoms while keeping logs and traces one click from the alert.

01Requirements

Functional
  • Ingest logs, metrics, and traces from services, gateways, background workers, and the edge.
  • Correlate all telemetry for one request across services by a shared trace or correlation ID.
  • Query and dashboard by service, endpoint, error type, or business entity over a time range.
  • Evaluate alert rules, deduplicate them into incidents, and route pages or tickets to the owning team.
  • Track SLOs and error budgets per user journey and per service.
  • Keep an incident timeline and an audit trail of who acknowledged, mitigated, and changed what.
Non-functional
  • Telemetry ingestion must not block or materially slow the request path; buffer and batch asynchronously.
  • Metrics should appear within seconds and logs within about a minute, fast enough to debug a live incident.
  • The observability stack needs its own blast radius; an outage in the systems it watches must not take it down.
  • Cost must stay bounded through sampling, low cardinality, compression, and tiered retention.
  • Sensitive data must be redacted before ingestion, with per-team access control and audit.
  • Alerts must survive collector restarts without duplicate pages or silently missed conditions.
  • Assume 50 services and 20k requests/s at peak, with one structured log line per request at about 1 KB: roughly 20 MB/s of raw logs before sampling, so success logs need sampling while errors are kept.
  • About 200 active metric series per service at a 15-60s scrape interval gives roughly 10k active series; adding user_id or request_id as a label would multiply that by orders of magnitude.
  • Keep 100 percent of traces for errors and slow requests and sample 1-5 percent of successful ones, at 20-50 spans per trace and a few hundred bytes per span.
  • Retention of 7-30 days hot and searchable, about 90 days in object storage, and 1 year for audit logs keeps the expensive tier small.
  • Aim for fewer than two pages per on-call shift, each with an owner, a runbook, and a dashboard link.

04Data modelling

DecisionChoiceWhy
Ingestion pathA per-host or sidecar agent buffers in memory and ships OTLP batches to a collector tierThe request path must never block on the observability backend, and a collector absorbs retries and protocol translation.
Metric storageA time-series store keyed by metric name plus a small fixed label set, never user_id or request_idLow cardinality keeps index size, query cost, and ingestion affordable; high-cardinality detail belongs in logs and traces.
Log storageAppend-only, time-partitioned storage with hot searchable days and cold object-storage tiersLogs are immutable, queried mainly by time and service, and older data is rarely read but must still exist for audits.
Trace storageSpan rows keyed by trace_id, head sampling for successes plus tail sampling for errors and slow requestsStoring every successful trace is expensive, while the requests that matter for debugging are the failures and outliers.
CorrelationGenerate trace_id at the gateway, propagate it with W3C traceparent and event attributes, and attach it to every log lineOne identifier joins logs, traces, and alert context without guessing from timestamps or user IDs.
Alert stateEvaluate rules in a stateless engine, keyed by a rule fingerprint plus a for-window, with dedupe and acknowledgement in a small KV storeThe engine can scale horizontally while duplicate notifications and repeated pages for one incident are suppressed.

05Architecture

Services emit logs, metrics, and spans through a collector into separate stores; the alert engine reads metrics and pages the owner, while dashboards join all three signals by trace ID.
  • Metrics detect, traces locate the slow hop, and logs explain the event; do not use one signal to do all three jobs.
  • Instrument at the boundaries first: inbound requests, outbound dependency calls, important state changes, and decisions such as a fallback or a tripped breaker.
  • Page on symptoms such as error-budget burn or failed checkouts; keep cause metrics on dashboards and as leading indicators, not as twenty separate pages.
  • Every alert carries the current value, threshold, window, owner, dashboard link, and runbook so the responder does not start by re-deriving the rule.
  • Run the observability stack with its own SLO and a dead-man switch; missing telemetry is a failure, not a quiet healthy system.
  • Propagate the trace ID through HTTP headers, queue messages, and scheduled jobs so asynchronous work stays attached to the request that caused it.

06Optimisation & trade-offs

DecisionGainCost / risk
Tail-based trace samplingKeeps complete traces for errors and slow requests, which are the ones investigators needThe collector must buffer spans until the trace completes, adding memory and pipeline complexity
Full log retentionAny past event can be reconstructed exactlyVolume grows with traffic and the hot search tier becomes the dominant bill
Rich metric labelsFaster slicing by region, endpoint, or tenantEach extra label multiplies series and can make the time-series store unusable
Symptom-based pagingRoughly one page per incident and pages that map to user painDetection can lag a known leading cause, so some failures are caught later
Short alert for-windowFast detection of a real outageBrief spikes page repeatedly unless paired with a longer confirmation window
One central observability stackA single search across every service and one place to manage accessIt becomes a shared dependency and a large blast radius if it fails
Managed observability serviceNo storage or indexing cluster to operatePer-GB ingest, data egress, lock-in, and data-residency constraints
Asynchronous log shippingProtects request latency from a slow or unavailable backendBuffered entries can be lost on a crash, so the buffer policy must be explicit
  • Tail-sample traces: keep every error and every request above the latency SLO, and sample routine successes aggressively.
  • Pre-aggregate at the collector with recording rules so dashboards and alerts read a few series instead of scanning raw events.
  • Use constant log messages with values in structured fields, so identical events group cleanly instead of creating a unique string per line.
  • Buffer, compress, and batch on ingest, and drop DEBUG first under backpressure rather than blocking the application.
  • Tier retention and delete on a schedule; audit logs can live for a year in cheap storage while debug logs expire in days.
  • Drop or aggregate labels that exceed a cardinality budget, and move the detail into exemplars or logs.
  • Link a metric bucket to a sample trace with an exemplar, so an alert jumps straight to a representative slow request.

07Follow-ups — one-line answers

Why not just collect logs? Metrics are cheap to aggregate and alert on, traces show where time went, and logs explain one event in detail; each answers a different question.
What is a correlation ID and where is it created? It is generated at the edge or gateway and propagated through headers and messages so every service can write it on every log line and one search reconstructs the request.
Should you page on symptoms or causes? Page on symptoms because they match user impact one-to-one, and use cause metrics as dashboard context or as leading indicators for failures that reliably precede impact.
What is an error budget? It is one minus the SLO: the amount of unreliability allowed in the window, spent on releases and risk and used to justify freezing changes when exhausted.
What is burn-rate alerting? It measures how fast the error budget is being consumed; a fast burn over a short window pages now, while a slow burn over a long window opens a ticket.
How do you control metric cardinality? Keep labels to a small, bounded set such as service, endpoint, region, and status; put user and request identifiers in logs or trace attributes instead.
How do you keep observability costs down? Sample success telemetry, keep all errors, pre-aggregate metrics, compress and batch at the agent, and tier retention from hot search to cheap object storage.
How do you reduce alert fatigue? Delete non-actionable rules, downgrade slow-burn conditions to tickets, add for-windows and hysteresis, deduplicate into incidents, and inhibit downstream pages during a known dependency outage.
How do you monitor the monitoring system? Use a dead-man switch or heartbeat on expected telemetry, synthetic checks from the user path, and alerts on collector lag, dropped batches, and missing series.
What happens if the collector is down? Agents buffer within a bounded disk or memory limit, shed DEBUG and INFO first, and raise a local alarm if the buffer is close to overflowing.
Head sampling or tail sampling? Head sampling is decided at the start and is cheap but can miss rare failures; tail sampling waits for the whole trace and keeps errors and slow requests at the cost of buffering.
What would you alert on for checkout? Checkout success rate and latency against the SLO, error-budget burn, and a drop in completed orders versus baseline, with dependency errors and queue depth attached as context.

08Traps

  • Paging on every CPU spike, disk at seventy percent, or other causes, so one incident produces many pages and responders stop trusting the pager.
  • Writing alert thresholds with no for-window, so every brief spike becomes an incident.
  • Putting user_id, request_id, or raw URLs in metric labels and then wondering why the time-series store melts.
  • Logging the same exception at every layer and alerting on the inflated error count.
  • Treating dashboards as alerting and assuming someone is watching when nobody knows an incident is happening.
  • Keeping every log forever in the expensive searchable tier and discovering the bill before discovering the outage.

Absorb Traffic Spikes

Interviewers ask it to separate candidates who say "autoscale" from those who can explain what happens in the first ten seconds, what gets rejected, and how the system recovers afterwards.

One-line answerA spike is about ramp speed, not average load: have capacity ready with headroom and pre-warming, cap demand with rate limits and admission control, buffer only in bounded queues, and shed or degrade so the critical flow survives.

01Requirements

Functional
  • Survive a sudden jump to many times the baseline request rate without a cascading failure.
  • Keep critical flows such as login, checkout, and payment working while optional features degrade.
  • Queue or defer work that can genuinely finish later, and tell the client it was accepted.
  • Reject excess traffic quickly with a clear error and a retry hint instead of a slow timeout.
  • Drain the backlog and return to normal latency after the peak.
  • Pre-scale and warm capacity for known events such as a sale or a launch.
Non-functional
  • p99 for admitted requests stays inside the target instead of climbing with the load.
  • Every buffer has a maximum depth and a maximum age; nothing queues without bound.
  • Autoscaling reacts in minutes, so step changes must be covered by headroom or pre-warming.
  • Degradation is a flag change, not a deploy.
  • Shed and reject decisions are counted, labelled, and alertable.
  • Retries are capped and jittered so a blip does not become a sustained overload.
  • Assume 5k requests per second at baseline and 100k per second for ten minutes during a flash sale, a 20x step change.
  • A new instance takes roughly 60 to 120 seconds to boot, pass health checks, and warm its cache, so a 10-second ramp cannot be met reactively.
  • Keeping 40 percent headroom above the expected peak costs about 1.4x baseline capacity while idle, which is usually cheaper than an outage.
  • A queue holding 1M messages at about 1 KB each is roughly 1 GB; draining at 20k messages per second takes about 50 seconds.
  • If one instance handles about 500 requests per second for this endpoint, a 100k peak needs about 200 instances before treating headroom as extra.

Defenses apply in order: the edge caps and caches, admission control protects the API tier, queues hold only deferrable work, and flags plus metrics drive degradation and scaling.
  • Order the defenses: capacity you already have, then admission at the door, then bounded buffering, then shedding and degradation. Capacity alone is not a strategy.
  • Autoscaling is a follower, not a shield. It helps sustained growth and hurts nothing, but a step change is absorbed by headroom and pre-warming.
  • Scale the bottleneck tier. Adding API instances while the database or a third-party API stays fixed just moves the queue.
  • New instances start cold: empty caches and empty pools push extra load onto the database exactly when it is weakest.
  • Queueing converts a latency failure into a backlog. It works only when consumers can eventually outpace producers.
  • Measure the recovery tail, not just the peak: time to drain the backlog and return p99 to baseline is the real recovery metric.

06Optimisation & trade-offs

DecisionGainCost / risk
Static headroomAbsorbs a step change immediately and survives a lost nodePays for idle capacity at baseline
Reactive autoscalingCheap at baseline and handles gradual growthReacts after minutes and starts cold instances that add load
Scheduled pre-warmingCapacity and caches are ready before a known eventDepends on an accurate forecast and adds temporary spend
Rate limiting and quotasCaps demand before it reaches shared resources and gives a fast, honest rejectionRejects legitimate users during a genuine surge
Bounded queue with backpressureConverts a burst into a drainable backlog and protects memoryAdds delay and duplicates, and still fails if consumers are persistently slower
Load sheddingKeeps most requests fast and protects the critical flowExplicitly fails some requests that might have succeeded
Graceful degradationCore journeys survive when optional features cannotUsers get a reduced or stale experience and need to be told
Waiting roomKeeps a high-demand event from collapsing the purchase pathAdds friction and a second stateful system to operate
  • Pre-warm before known events: scale the bottleneck, warm caches, open pools, and raise provider quotas in advance.
  • Run 30 to 50 percent headroom on the bottleneck tier so one lost node or one surge does not saturate it.
  • Serve public reads from the CDN with stale-while-revalidate so a surge never forces synchronous revalidation.
  • Apply admission control at the edge with per-route concurrency caps, not just per-IP rate limits.
  • Bound every queue, attach a deadline, and reject or drop work that can no longer be useful.
  • Return 429 with a Retry-After hint and jittered client backoff instead of letting requests time out.
  • Put circuit breakers and aggressive timeouts in front of every dependency, and isolate connection pools per dependency.
  • Use flags to disable recommendations, live counters, and personalisation first, keeping the purchase path intact.

07Follow-ups — one-line answers

Why is autoscaling not enough for a ticket on-sale? The spike ramps in seconds while scaling takes minutes to launch, pass health checks, and warm caches, so pre-warming and admission control carry the peak.
Headroom or autoscaling? Headroom for sharp, short, or unpredictable spikes; autoscaling for gradual or sustained growth, and most systems run both.
When does a queue help and when does it only hide the problem? It helps a short burst that consumers can later drain; it hides the problem when consumers are persistently slower, so lag grows without limit.
What is backpressure and how do you implement it? A signal that producers must slow down or retry later, implemented with bounded queues, concurrency limits, and explicit rejection when the buffer is full.
What would you shed first? Bots, background jobs, analytics, and expensive non-critical features, while protecting login, checkout, and payment with their own limits.
How do you prevent a retry storm? Retry only idempotent calls, cap attempts, use exponential backoff with jitter, respect Retry-After, and put a circuit breaker in front of failing dependencies.
How do you protect the database during a spike? Cap API concurrency, cache aggressively, queue writes, use read replicas for non-critical reads, and shed or degrade anything that touches the primary.
When would you use a waiting room? For a known event where demand far exceeds capacity but users will wait, such as a ticket sale, admitting them at a rate the purchase path can serve.
Why do cold instances make a spike worse? They start with empty caches and no established connections, so their first requests hit the database and dependency pools that are already the bottleneck.
How do you test for a spike before it happens? Load test above the expected peak with a realistic ramp shape and dependency latency, then run a game day that exercises shedding, degradation, and recovery.
What do you monitor during and after the peak? Rps, p95 and p99, error and reject rates, saturation, queue depth and age, cache hit ratio, autoscaler activity, and time back to baseline.
What if the spike lands on one key or one tenant? That is a hot-key problem, not a capacity problem: add per-key limits, cache or replicate the key, and isolate the tenant before scaling the whole fleet.

08Traps

  • Saying autoscaling will handle it, without explaining the seconds before the first new instance is ready.
  • Provisioning for the daily average and calling that number peak capacity.
  • Leaving queues unbounded and treating a growing backlog as success.
  • Retrying aggressively without backoff or jitter and turning a blip into an outage.
  • Scaling every tier except the actual bottleneck, usually the database or a third party.
  • Applying the same rate limit to payment and login traffic as to browsing.

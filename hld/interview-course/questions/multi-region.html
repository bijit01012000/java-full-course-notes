Run Across Multiple Regions

Multi-region is the usual follow-up to 'make it highly available', and it forces you to reason about latency, replication, and who owns writes. Interviewers listen for a topology chosen from RPO and RTO, not a reflex answer of active-active everywhere.

One-line answerRoute users to the nearest healthy region, keep one writer per data partition, replicate asynchronously with a measured RPO, and fail over only with a quorum, fencing, and a rehearsed runbook.

01Requirements

Functional
  • Route each request to the nearest healthy region using GeoDNS, anycast, or a global load balancer, and move traffic away from a failed region.
  • Serve reads from a local replica and route writes to the partition's home region.
  • Replicate user data, search indexes, and object assets across regions with a stated RPO and monitored lag.
  • Promote a standby region or transfer home-region ownership during a regional failure, then fail back.
  • Keep regulated data inside its jurisdiction while exposing only derived or aggregated data globally.
  • Expose per-region health, replication lag, and failover state to operators.
Non-functional
  • Regional read p95 stays inside the product latency budget; a cross-region write pays the WAN round trip, so keep it off the common path.
  • Target RPO under one minute and RTO under fifteen minutes for a warm standby, verified by drills rather than assumed.
  • Survive the loss of one region with N+1 capacity at peak, including shared dependencies such as identity, DNS, and config.
  • Use per-data consistency: strong for money and inventory, read-your-writes for profiles, eventual for feeds and counters.
  • Prevent split brain with a quorum, a leader lease, and fencing so an old primary cannot accept writes after a new epoch.
  • Keep PII in its home region, and treat encryption keys and audit logs as data with their own residency rules.
  • Assume 20M DAU and 10 requests per user per day: about 200M requests/day, roughly 2.3k average RPS and 7k peak at 3x.
  • Assume 5% writes: about 115 average write RPS and 350 peak, so most traffic is a read-locality problem rather than a write-scaling problem.
  • At 2 KB per response, 200M responses/day is about 400 GB/day of egress before replication and backups.
  • Assume 500 GB of hot relational data per region plus 5 TB of object assets; replicate the write stream, not full scans.
  • Cross-region round trips are tens to low hundreds of milliseconds depending on distance; budget the RTT explicitly instead of hiding it in a timeout.

04Data modelling

Write topologyShard-per-region with a home region per user; reads are served locallyOne writer per key avoids concurrent-write conflicts, and most user data is read by users near that same region.
Cross-region replicationAsynchronous WAL or change-stream replication; synchronous only across availability zones inside a regionSynchronous replication across an ocean adds RTT to every write and blocks when the far side is unreachable; async keeps writes local at the cost of a non-zero RPO.

Traffic routingGeoDNS or a global load balancer with latency-based and failover routing, with short TTLs on failover recordsRouting is simple and cache-friendly, but resolver and client caching still delay some clients, so the application must tolerate a stale route.
Data residencyPartition by jurisdiction and replicate only derived or aggregated data globallyThis keeps PII inside the allowed geography and limits the blast radius of a cross-border replication mistake.
Failover authorityAn external coordinator with quorum; leases and epochs stored in a strongly consistent storeA region cannot safely promote itself, and the old leader must be unable to write once a new epoch begins.

Reads are served by the local region, writes land in the partition's home region, and the failover controller promotes a standby only after acquiring a new lease and fencing the old writer.
  • Multi-AZ comes first: it handles most facility and hardware failures for far less complexity than a second region.
  • Keep request paths regional; a write that fans out to three regions multiplies latency and failure modes.
  • Keep the standby warm with capacity, connection pools, and caches, because an autoscaling group cannot absorb a full region instantly.
  • Shared dependencies such as identity, DNS, config, and third-party providers need their own regional story or the extra region does not help.
  • Deploy region by region and keep schema and API changes backward compatible, since a worldwide rollout is never atomic.

06Optimisation & trade-offs

DecisionGainCost / risk
Active-passive warm standbySimple single-writer data model and lower cost than running every region at full production capacity.Idle capacity, minutes of downtime during promotion, and a non-zero RPO.
Active-activeLocal writes and instant traffic shifting when a region fails.Conflict resolution for every writable dataset and much harder testing and operations.
Asynchronous replicationFast local writes that do not depend on far-region health.A non-zero RPO and stale reads from regions that have not caught up.
Home-region ownershipConflict-free writes and simple per-user ordering.A migration and ownership-transfer problem when that region fails.
Short DNS TTLsFaster traffic failover after a regional failure.More DNS query volume, and clients that ignore TTL still stall regardless.
N+1 regional headroomThe surviving regions can carry peak traffic after losing one region.Expensive capacity that sits idle most of the time.
Global strong consistencySimple correctness reasoning for every read and write.Cross-region coordination on the hot path and poor availability during a partition.
  • Serve reads from a local replica and use a per-user version token only when read-your-writes is required.
  • Keep caches regional; a global cache adds a cross-region hop and a harder invalidation problem.
  • Replicate the change stream in batches and compress it; avoid application-level dual writes that can diverge.
  • Pre-warm standby caches, runtimes, and connection pools before shifting traffic.
  • Use request coalescing and load shedding in the surviving region so failover traffic does not cause a second outage.
  • Send only critical writes across regions and leave non-critical replication asynchronous.
  • Track p99 latency by region and replication lag as first-class SLIs, not just aggregate availability.

07Follow-ups — one-line answers

Why would you run a system in multiple regions? To cut latency for distant users, survive a full regional outage, meet a short RTO, or satisfy data residency; name the requirement before choosing the topology.
What is the difference between multi-AZ and multi-region? Multi-AZ survives data-center failures inside one region, while multi-region survives losing the region but adds WAN latency and a data-consistency problem.
Active-active versus active-passive? Active-active serves writes everywhere and needs conflict handling, while active-passive keeps one writer and a standby that is promoted on failure.
How do you route users to a region? Use GeoDNS, anycast, or a global load balancer with latency and failover routing, keep failover TTLs short, and accept that some clients cache longer.
What are RPO and RTO? RPO is the write window you can afford to lose and RTO is how long recovery may take; both drive the replication mode and must be measured in drills.
How do you keep data consistent across regions? Choose per data type: strong for money and inventory, read-your-writes for profiles, and eventual consistency for feeds and counters.
How do you resolve write conflicts? Avoid them with home-region ownership, otherwise detect concurrency with version vectors and merge by business rule instead of blind last-write-wins.
How do you prevent split brain during failover? Require quorum to promote, fence the old primary with a lease epoch, and reject writes from a leader that cannot prove it still holds the lease.
Why is failback harder than failover? Both regions may have accepted writes, so data must be reconciled, caches and indexes rebuilt, replication reversed, and traffic shifted gradually.
How much spare capacity does a region need? Size the surviving regions for peak plus the failed region's load, which usually means N+1 capacity that sits idle in normal operation.
When is a single region enough? When multi-AZ plus tested backups meets the availability target and users are not latency-sensitive across continents, and saying so is a strong answer.
What would you monitor in production? Per-region latency and errors, replication lag, routing health, failover readiness, and headroom for region loss, with observability that survives a region outage.

08Traps

  • Adding a second region without deciding how writes behave when both regions are reachable.
  • Calling the design active-active while every write still goes to one region.
  • Using last-write-wins on valuable data and calling the lost update resolved.
  • Sizing the standby for normal load instead of the load it must carry after a failure.
  • Promoting a lagging replica without stating the data loss or fencing the old primary.
  • Ignoring global dependencies such as identity, DNS, config, and third-party APIs, or assuming DNS failover is instant despite client caching.

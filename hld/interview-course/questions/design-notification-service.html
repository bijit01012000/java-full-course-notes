Design a Notification Service

It looks simple until you add multiple channels, provider limits, retries, and user preferences, so it tests asynchronous pipeline design and duplicate control.

One-line answerAccept a notification request idempotently, persist it with an outbox, route it through per-channel queues with preference and rate-limit checks, and retry provider calls with backoff into a dead-letter queue.

01Requirements

Functional
  • Accept notification requests from internal services and deliver over push, email, SMS, and in-app channels.
  • Render localized templates with per-user variables and support scheduling for a future time.
  • Respect user preferences, channel opt-outs, category subscriptions, and quiet hours.
  • Deduplicate and collapse repeated notifications, for example ten likes becoming one 'and 9 others' message.
  • Retry transient provider failures, fail over where possible, and expose per-notification delivery status.
  • Provide a dead-letter queue and a replay path for notifications that exhaust retries.
Non-functional
  • Accept and enqueue a request in under about 100ms at p99; actual delivery is asynchronous.
  • Guarantee at-least-once delivery and make duplicate arrivals harmless to the user.
  • Isolate channels so a slow email provider cannot block push notifications.
  • Sustain roughly 1B notifications per day with a peak several times the average.
  • Respect provider rate limits and back off instead of hammering a failing downstream.
  • Keep an auditable record of what was sent, through which provider, and what happened.
  • Assume 100M users and 1B notifications per day: about 12k per second average and 50k per second at peak.
  • Assume 70 percent push, 20 percent email, and 10 percent SMS; each channel has different latency, cost, and provider quotas.
  • At an average payload of 1KB, raw notification records are about 1TB per day before indexes and retention, so partition by time and archive old rows.
  • A user with 5 devices needs 5 push attempts for one logical notification, so device fan-out multiplies provider calls even when the notification count does not change.
  • Retries can multiply traffic during a provider incident; cap attempts, use jitter, and route to a fallback only for critical categories.

04Data modelling

DecisionChoiceWhy
Notification record and outboxA notification table partitioned by created_at with a unique idempotency_key, plus an outbox row written in the same transaction; a relay publishes outbox rows and marks them sent.Time partitioning keeps the hot table small, the unique key makes producer retries safe, and the outbox prevents a committed notification from being lost when the queue publish fails.
Queue layoutSeparate queues per channel, and within a channel separate high and low priority. Partition by user_id for per-user ordering where needed.A slow or rate-limited provider then affects only its own channel, and priority keeps password resets ahead of marketing email.
PreferencesA compact per-user preference row keyed by user and category, cached in Redis with a version, and checked before enqueue and again before send.Preferences change rarely but are read on every notification; a cached read keeps the accept path fast while the send-time check catches recent opt-outs.
Dedupe and collapseA Redis key per user, category, and time window with a short TTL, plus a collapse key for notifications that can be merged.Most duplicates arrive within seconds; a TTL window catches retries and event storms without an unbounded permanent table, and collapse prevents notification fatigue.
Attempt historyAppend one delivery_attempt row per provider call and keep the provider message id and error code.Support needs to prove whether a message was sent, which provider handled it, and why it failed; an append-only record also supports reconciliation with provider reports.
SchedulingStore scheduled_at in UTC and use a durable delayed-job store plus a poller; do not rely on an in-process timer.A reminder may be hours or days away and must survive deploys and crashes, so the schedule has to live outside the process.

Requests are checked against preferences and dedupe, stored with an outbox, then routed into per-channel queues whose workers call providers and record every attempt; provider callbacks close the status loop.
  • Return 202 as soon as the notification is durably stored. Delivery latency is a provider concern; making the producer wait couples its availability to email and SMS vendors.
  • Check preferences before enqueue for fast rejection, and check again before send because a user may opt out while the notification sits in a queue or waits for quiet hours to end.
  • Use one queue per channel and priority. A single shared queue lets an email provider outage back up password-reset pushes behind marketing campaigns.
  • Workers must be idempotent: persist the attempt id, pass a stable idempotency key to the provider where supported, and treat a provider duplicate response as success for the same attempt.
  • Retry only transient failures such as timeouts, 429s, and 5xx responses. Do not retry invalid tokens, hard bounces, or malformed templates; send those to a terminal state or the DLQ.
  • Use exponential backoff with jitter and a maximum attempt count. A provider outage should reduce traffic, not create a retry storm that keeps it down.
  • Provider webhooks are at-least-once and can arrive out of order. Verify the signature, dedupe by provider event id, and apply status transitions only when they move forward.
  • Quiet hours defer non-critical notifications to the user's local morning; critical security and transactional messages should have an explicit override policy.

06Optimisation & trade-offs

DecisionGainCost / risk
Fully asynchronous pipelineProducers are decoupled from provider latency and outages, and bursts are absorbed by bounded queues.Status is eventually consistent, retries and dedupe become necessary, and the user may wait seconds longer for a message.
Per-channel queuesFailure isolation and independent scaling for push, email, and SMS, with channel-specific rate limits.More queues and workers to operate, and a single notification that needs multiple channels requires coordination across them.
At-least-once deliveryA crash or timeout does not silently drop a notification, which is the more visible failure for transactional messages.Users can receive duplicates, so every send path needs an idempotency key or a dedupe window.
Short dedupe window in RedisFast and cheap protection against retries and event storms without an ever-growing database table.A duplicate arriving after the TTL is not caught, and Redis loss temporarily widens the duplicate window.
Immediate send for transactional categoriesPassword resets and payment alerts arrive while they are still useful.It ignores quiet hours and can wake users, so the policy must distinguish critical from marketing messages.
Provider abstraction with failoverA single vendor outage does not stop a channel, and routing can optimize for cost or deliverability.Templates and status codes differ across providers, and failover can send a duplicate if the first attempt actually succeeded.
Persist every delivery attemptAuditable status, easier support, and a basis for reconciling provider invoices and complaints.Write volume grows with retries and devices, so retention and partitioning are mandatory.
  • Batch and pipeline provider calls where the provider supports it, rather than one HTTP request per recipient.
  • Cache templates, preferences, and device tokens so the worker path does not make several database reads per send.
  • Collapse high-frequency categories into a digest or a single grouped notification to reduce volume and user fatigue.
  • Route by cost and reliability: push before SMS, primary provider before fallback, and cheap channels for low-priority categories.
  • Use token buckets per provider and per channel so rate limits are respected globally rather than discovered through 429 responses.
  • Partition the notification table by day and archive completed rows; keep only recent status lookups hot.
  • Precompute segment membership for campaigns and enqueue in chunks, so a large campaign does not create one giant transaction.
  • Track delivery, bounce, and complaint rates per provider and switch traffic away from a degrading provider before it fails completely.

07Follow-ups — one-line answers

How do you prevent duplicate notifications? Require an idempotency key per logical notification, enforce it with a unique constraint, and add a short per-user category dedupe window for event storms.
What happens when a provider is down? Retries back off with jitter, the circuit breaker opens, and only critical categories fail over to a secondary provider while everything else queues or dead-letters.
How do you respect provider rate limits? Use a shared token bucket per provider and channel, and let workers pull at the permitted rate instead of discovering the limit through 429 responses.
Does notification ordering matter? Rarely globally, but per user and category it can: partition those queues by user_id so a 'resolved' message does not overtake the alert it refers to.
How do quiet hours work? Store the user's timezone and preferred window, defer non-critical sends until the window opens, and allow an explicit override for security and transactional messages.
What do you do with a permanently invalid push token? Mark the device token invalid from the provider response, stop using it, and prompt the app to register a fresh token on next launch.
How do you fan out a campaign to 10M users? Resolve the segment in pages, enqueue bounded batches, and let channel workers drain at provider rate rather than creating 10M synchronous sends.
How is delivery status tracked? Each attempt records its provider message id, and signed provider webhooks update the attempt and notification state with deduplication by event id.
What happens after retries are exhausted? The notification goes to a dead-letter queue with its error context, alerts fire if the rate is abnormal, and an operator can replay it after the provider recovers.
How do users unsubscribe? A preference update disables the category or channel immediately, and the send path rechecks it so queued messages do not bypass the choice.
Push or email for a critical alert? Push first for immediacy, then fall back to email or SMS when the push is not delivered within a short deadline and the category is marked critical.
How do you avoid a notification storm during an incident? Aggregate related alerts, rate-limit per user and category, suppress duplicates, and have a global kill switch per category.

08Traps

  • Publishing to the queue before the database commit, which can lose a notification when the publish succeeds and the commit fails, or vice versa.
  • Retrying every error, including invalid device tokens and hard bounces, which wastes provider quota and never succeeds.
  • Using one shared queue for all channels, so a slow email vendor blocks urgent push and SMS traffic.
  • Checking preferences only at enqueue time, then sending a queued message after the user has opted out.
  • Claiming exactly-once delivery across an external provider, which is not achievable; the correct claim is effectively once via idempotency and dedupe.
  • Letting retries run without a cap or jitter during a provider outage, turning a vendor incident into a self-inflicted traffic spike.

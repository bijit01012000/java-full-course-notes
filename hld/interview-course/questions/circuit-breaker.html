Circuit Breaker and Bulkhead

It tests whether you understand failure propagation: how one slow dependency exhausts threads and turns a partial degradation into a full outage, and which mechanisms actually bound the damage.

One-line answerWrap each remote dependency in a per-dependency state machine that trips on failure or slow-call rate, rejects fast with a safe fallback, probes recovery in half-open, and caps concurrency with a bulkhead and retry budget.

01Requirements

Functional
  • Wrap outbound calls per dependency and endpoint with a timeout, circuit breaker, bulkhead, and bounded retries.
  • Trip from closed to open when failure rate or slow-call rate crosses a threshold over a sliding window with enough volume.
  • While open, reject immediately or return the configured fallback without calling the dependency.
  • After a cool-down, admit a small number of half-open probes; close on success and reopen on any probe failure.
  • Expose state, transitions, rejected calls, fallback rate, and probe results as metrics and events.
  • Support per-dependency and per-tenant policy, forced-open for maintenance, and runtime configuration changes.
Non-functional
  • The per-call decision must be local and cheap, with no network hop on the request path.
  • Rejection must be fast, bounded by the timeout, and never wait on the failing dependency.
  • State updates must be safe under high concurrency and must not lose counts under contention.
  • Policy changes must apply without a redeploy, and a missing or invalid policy must fall back to safe defaults.
  • Fail-open or fail-closed behavior must be chosen per dependency based on business impact, not globally.
  • Breaker and bulkhead state must be observable enough to explain why traffic was rejected.
  • Assume checkout peaks at 2k requests/s and each request makes one payment call; with a 500 ms timeout, a 200-thread pool is consumed by about 400 hung calls per second, so the breaker must trip in seconds rather than minutes.
  • Starting policy: a 10s sliding window, a minimum of 20 calls, a 50 percent failure-rate threshold, a 1s slow-call threshold, a 2s call timeout, and an open duration of 30s.
  • Allow 3 half-open probe calls and jitter the open timer by 0 to 10s so a fleet does not probe in one synchronized wave.
  • Cap the dependency bulkhead at about 50 concurrent calls per instance, well below the thread or connection pool, so one dependency cannot consume the whole service.
  • Cap retries at roughly 10 percent of successful traffic as a retry budget, with exponential backoff and jitter.

04Data modelling

DecisionChoiceWhy
Breaker stateIn-process memory per instance, with an atomic state enum and countersThe decision must be a local read on the hot path; per-instance state is expected and cheaper than coordinating across the fleet.
Sliding windowA ring of one-second buckets holding success, failure, and slow-call countsIt gives bounded memory and recent behavior instead of a lifetime average that reacts hours too late.
Policy configurationVersioned central config pushed to instances and cached locally as last-known-goodAll instances should agree on thresholds without a per-call lookup, and a config outage must not disable protection.
TransitionsAppend-only transition events plus a current-state gauge, each with a reasonFlapping and repeated opens are easiest to diagnose from the history, correlated with dependency latency and errors.
Fallback cacheA separate cache with a short TTL and an explicit degraded flagThe fallback must not call the failing dependency, and an entry that never expires can hide a permanent failure.
BulkheadA semaphore per dependency per instance, independent of the breakerThe breaker stops calls after detecting failure, while the bulkhead prevents one dependency from taking every thread in the first place.

Calls pass through a per-dependency bulkhead and breaker; failures and slow calls feed the sliding window, which opens the breaker, sends traffic to a fallback, and later admits a few half-open probes.
  • Use the full chain in order: a timeout shorter than the caller deadline, a per-dependency bulkhead, the breaker, bounded retries, and a deliberate fallback.
  • Scope the breaker to one dependency and usually one endpoint or cluster; a global breaker lets a failing recommendation service block checkout.
  • Do not retry when the breaker is open; an open breaker plus retries turns fast rejection into a delayed retry loop.
  • Treat slow calls as failures because a dependency that answers successfully after many seconds still consumes the caller's capacity.
  • Per-instance breakers mean the fleet is never perfectly uniform; aggregate state across instances before declaring recovery, and jitter the open timer.
  • In a service mesh the breaker is uniform across languages but sees routes rather than business operations; choose one owner per hop for timeouts, retries, and breakers.

06Optimisation & trade-offs

DecisionGainCost / risk
Fail fast when openPreserves threads and connections so the caller and unrelated endpoints stay availableTurns a slow dependency into an immediate user-visible error or a degraded response
Aggressive thresholdsTrips early and protects capacity during a real outageFalse positives during normal variance, which reject traffic that would have succeeded
Lenient thresholdsAvoids tripping on noiseA degraded dependency can exhaust the caller before protection engages
Per-instance breaker stateSimple, local, and fast with no coordinationThe fleet view is inconsistent and some instances keep sending traffic while others are open
Mesh-level breakerUniform policy across languages and servicesCoarser than business semantics and unable to distinguish a business failure from a transport failure
Cached fallbackKeeps a usable page or estimate when the dependency is downCan serve stale or incorrect data, especially if the entry has no expiry
Retry budgetBounds amplification during an incidentRejects some requests that might have recovered on a retry
Short open windowRestores service quickly when the dependency recoversHammers a dependency that is still down and causes repeated flapping
  • Tune the slow-call threshold between the dependency's normal p99 and the timeout, and revisit it after traffic or dependency changes.
  • Use bucketed counters or atomics for the sliding window instead of a global lock on every call.
  • Jitter the open duration and keep half-open probes to a handful so recovery does not become a thundering herd.
  • Serve a cached fallback with a short TTL and mark the response as degraded, both in the payload and in metrics.
  • Retry only transient, retryable, idempotent operations, at exactly one layer, with exponential backoff and jitter.
  • Split breakers by endpoint, cluster, or tenant where one bad route or noisy tenant would otherwise disable healthy traffic.
  • Precompute and atomically swap the policy snapshot so policy reads cost nothing at call time.

07Follow-ups — one-line answers

Why is a timeout not enough? A timeout bounds one call, but every doomed call still consumes its full duration, so at high volume the pool still fills; the breaker stops issuing calls entirely for a cool-down.
Explain the three states. Closed passes calls and records outcomes, open rejects immediately for a cool-down, and half-open admits a few probes that either close the breaker or reopen it.
Why count slow calls as failures? A dependency can return success too slowly to be usable, and those slow calls still hold threads, so counting them lets the breaker trip before the caller is exhausted.
Why is a minimum request volume needed? Without it, two failures out of two calls trip a low-traffic dependency, causing constant flapping instead of protection.
What is a good fallback for payment versus recommendations? Recommendations can serve a cached list or disappear from the page, while payment must return an explicit failure or queue the attempt, never a fake success.
Breaker, bulkhead, rate limiter: what is the difference? A breaker stops calls after failures are detected, a bulkhead caps how many resources one dependency can consume, and a rate limiter caps request rate over time.
Is breaker state shared across the fleet? Usually not; each instance keeps its own state, so aggregate metrics across instances and accept that recovery is gradual and slightly uneven.
How do you avoid a thundering herd in half-open? Jitter the open timer and admit only a few probe calls, so the fleet does not all test the dependency at the same instant.
Where should the breaker live? In-process when business semantics matter, in a sidecar or mesh for uniform cross-language policy, or at the gateway for north-south traffic, with one owner per hop.
How do breakers and retries interact? Retries add load when the dependency is weakest, so retry only transient idempotent failures, cap attempts with a budget, and suppress retries while the breaker is open.
How do you test a breaker? Inject latency and errors in staging, verify it trips at the configured threshold, check the fallback, and confirm it closes cleanly when the dependency recovers.
How do you decide fail-open or fail-closed? Fail open only when the feature is non-critical and a safe degraded answer exists; fail closed for payments, authorization, and anything where a wrong success is worse than an error.

08Traps

  • Adding a breaker without a timeout, so slow calls never produce a bounded outcome to count.
  • Using one global breaker for all dependencies, letting a broken recommendation service block checkout.
  • Tripping on a single failure with no minimum volume, which produces constant flapping.
  • Retrying at the gateway, the sidecar, and the application at once, multiplying attempts during an outage.
  • Returning fake success from a fallback for a payment or other critical operation.
  • Assuming breaker state is fleet-wide when it is usually per instance.

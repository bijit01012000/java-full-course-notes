Distributed Transactions / SAGA

One-line answerKeep the work in one local transaction if you can; use 2PC only for short internal writes; otherwise run a saga of local transactions with idempotent compensations, an outbox for reliable events, and explicit recovery for stuck workflows.

01Requirements

Functional
  • Place an order that creates the order, reserves stock, charges payment, and books shipment.
  • On any step failure, leave no charge without an order and no stock held forever.
  • Tolerate client retries and duplicate message delivery without duplicating effects.
  • Expose the workflow's current state and history to users and support.
  • Allow cancellation and a manual repair path for irrecoverable cases.
  • Publish a business event reliably whenever state changes.
Non-functional
  • No distributed lock is held across a user-facing request or a third-party call.
  • Every step and every compensation is idempotent and safe to retry.
  • Intermediate state is either invisible to unrelated readers or explicitly marked as pending.
  • A stuck workflow is detected within minutes by a deadline, not discovered later by support.
  • The consistency guarantee is stated precisely: atomic, eventually consistent, or consistent within a window.
  • Money and inventory movements are auditable and reconcilable.
  • Assume 5k orders per second at peak and four participating services, so about 20k local transactions per second across the system.
  • 2PC would hold participant locks for tens of milliseconds to seconds; at 5k orders per second that is thousands of concurrent locks and a hard throughput ceiling.
  • A saga of four steps at roughly 100 to 300 ms per step gives a p99 end-to-end around 1 to 2 seconds when everything is healthy.
  • Assume 0.1 percent of sagas need compensation, which is still about 5 per second at peak and must not page anyone.
  • An outbox at 5k rows per second needs continuous purge; keeping it under about a million rows keeps relay lag and index cost small.

03API design

MethodPathPurpose
POST/ordersStarts the workflow with an Idempotency-Key and returns 202 with the order id and saga id.
GET/orders/{id}Returns order status including intermediate states such as awaiting_payment or compensating.
POST/orders/{id}/cancelRequests cancellation, which triggers compensation of completed steps rather than a rollback.
POST/internal/reservationsTry phase: holds stock for an order without committing it, with an expiry.
POST/internal/reservations/{id}/confirmConfirm phase: turns the hold into a permanent allocation, idempotently.
GET/internal/sagas/{id}Ops view of step state, attempts, and last error, used by dashboards and manual repair.

The orchestrator drives local transactions in each service, the outbox and relay publish events reliably, and a reconciliation path catches sagas and compensations that cannot complete.
  • First question: can the data live in one service? A single local transaction is always simpler than any distributed protocol, so check before designing one.
  • 2PC gives atomicity but holds locks across the network and blocks when the coordinator dies, so it fits only short, internal, few-participant writes.
  • A saga trades atomicity for availability; the steps are real commits, so other readers can see intermediate state and compensations must exist for every reversible step.
  • Ordering matters: put irreversible actions such as shipping or sending last, after all fallible checks have passed.
  • The outbox is what makes the saga reliable; without it, a crash between commit and publish loses the event that drives the next step.
  • The most common real failure is not an error but a workflow that never finishes, so every step needs a timeout, a deadline, and a dead-letter or manual path.

06Optimisation & trade-offs

DecisionGainCost / risk
Single local transactionReal atomicity and isolation with the least codeOnly possible when the data can live in one service and one database
Two-phase commitAtomic commit across participants without writing compensation logicLocks held across the network, low throughput, and blocking if the coordinator fails
Saga with compensationsAvailability and progress across long workflows and third partiesEventual consistency, visible intermediate states, and extra compensation code paths
OrchestrationOne place shows the flow, with explicit and testable failure logicA central dependency that can accumulate business logic
ChoreographyTeams decouple and there is no central bottleneckThe end-to-end flow is implicit and hard to trace or change
Try-confirm-cancel reservationsScarce resources are protected from late failureHolds reduce availability and need expiry handling
Transactional outboxEvents are never lost between commit and publishAn extra table, a relay, cleanup, and at-least-once delivery

  • Co-locate the steps in one service or database where the business allows it, removing the distributed transaction entirely.
  • Persist saga state and step status so recovery resumes at the current step instead of replaying from the start.
  • Give every step and compensation a stable idempotency key stored with its effect.
  • Use try-confirm-cancel reservations for scarce inventory so a late failure does not oversell.
  • Use an outbox with a relay or change data capture instead of writing the database and the broker separately.
  • Add a semantic lock or pending status so other transactions skip in-flight records.
  • Partition by order id to keep one entity's events ordered, and route poison messages to a DLQ with context.
  • Run reconciliation for the gap between orders created, charged, and fulfilled, and alert on the gap rather than on individual errors.

07Follow-ups — one-line answers

Why can't you use one database transaction across services? Atomicity and isolation are enforced by one database's transaction manager, and separate services have separate databases, connections, and failure domains, while external providers cannot participate at all.
What is two-phase commit and when would you use it? A coordinator asks every participant to prepare and lock, then commits only if all vote yes; it fits short, internal, few-participant writes with a hard atomicity requirement.
What is a saga? A sequence of local transactions where each completed step has a compensating action, so a later failure triggers business-level undo in reverse order and the system settles into eventual consistency.
Orchestration or choreography? Orchestration for complex flows with timeouts and many compensations because the logic is in one testable place; choreography for simple, loosely coupled reactions where teams want independence.
What makes a good compensating transaction? It reverses the business effect rather than a row, is idempotent, can be retried, and is ordered so irreversible actions happen last.
What if a compensation also fails? Retry it, then park the saga in a dead-letter or manual review queue with full context and alert, rather than leaving silent inconsistency.
What are saga isolation anomalies? Dirty reads of uncommitted saga state, lost updates, and fuzzy reads, countered with semantic locks, pending states, and version checks.
What is a semantic lock? A pending flag or status that marks a record as part of an in-flight saga so other operations wait, reject, or handle it specially.
Why do you need an outbox? Because the database commit and the event publish are separate operations, so writing the event in the same transaction makes publication reliable.
How do you avoid a double refund? Give the compensation a stable idempotency key recorded with its effect, or move the payment through a one-way state transition that can only happen once.
How do you detect a stuck saga? Every step gets a timeout and the saga gets a deadline, and a job alerts on instances past that deadline without a terminal state.
What do you monitor? Saga completion rate and duration, stuck count, compensation rate, outbox and DLQ lag, and the gap between orders created, charged, and fulfilled.

08Traps

  • Proposing 2PC for a workflow that calls an external payment provider or spans regions.
  • Describing a saga as a rollback, when compensations are separate business operations with their own failure modes.
  • Forgetting that saga steps are real commits and other readers can see or act on intermediate state.
  • Assuming compensations always succeed, with no dead-letter queue or manual reconciliation path.
  • Publishing events after the commit without an outbox, then losing the event when the process crashes.
  • Making a step non-idempotent so a retry charges the card twice, or leaving the irreversible step early in the sequence.

Headroom
Spare capacity deliberately kept above expected peak load so the system can absorb a surge or a node failure.
Autoscaling
Adding or removing instances based on observed load, which reacts after a delay and cannot handle an instantaneous surge alone.

Rate limiting
Capping how many requests a client, key, or endpoint may make in a time window.
Backpressure
Telling producers to slow down or retry later when consumers or buffers cannot keep up.

Serving a reduced but useful response when a dependency or feature is overloaded.

A queue in front of the system that admits users at a controlled rate during an extreme event.

01What a traffic spike is

Most of the time a system runs comfortably within its capacity. A spike is a sudden jump to many times the normal level, caused by an event rather than gradual growth.

  • A spike is characterized by magnitude, ramp speed, and duration, not just a higher average.
  • It can be planned, such as a ticket sale or a scheduled promotion, or unplanned, such as breaking news.
  • It can be concentrated on a few endpoints or a few keys rather than spread evenly.
  • It can be mostly reads, mostly writes, or a mix; each stresses a different part of the system.
  • The system must survive the peak and then return to a healthy state without a long recovery tail.
  • Spikes are a capacity and control problem, not only a scaling problem.

02Why spikes break healthy systems

  • Requests arrive faster than they can be served, so they queue; queueing raises latency even before capacity is exhausted.
  • Latency increases hold connections and memory longer, which reduces effective capacity further.
  • Thread pools, connection pools, and worker queues fill up, and new work is rejected or waits indefinitely.
  • Timeouts trigger retries, and retries add load to a system that is already saturated.
  • Clients that retry in lockstep create a thundering herd that can outlast the original trigger.
  • Autoscaling reacts after a delay, and new instances start cold with empty caches and no warm connections.
  • A saturated dependency can fail fast, but a saturated queue usually fails slowly, which is harder to recover from.
  • The failure is rarely isolated: one slow tier propagates latency and errors to every caller.
Traffic jumps→Queues grow→Latency rises→Timeouts and retries→More load on saturated tiers→Cascading failure

03Where this pattern shows up

  • A concert or ticket on-sale where thousands of users arrive at the exact start time.
  • A flash sale with a fixed start and a limited inventory.
  • A breaking news event that sends a large audience to the same article or live page.
  • A viral post or celebrity announcement that concentrates traffic on a few keys.
  • A livestream or live sports event where viewership ramps within seconds.
  • A scheduled batch or cron job that wakes up many workers at once.
  • A retry storm or reconnect storm after a brief outage or deploy.
  • A bot or scraping surge that looks like legitimate traffic but has no real user value.

04Characterize the spike before designing

The right response depends on the shape of the spike. Clarify these properties before choosing techniques.

  • How large is the peak relative to normal traffic, and how quickly does it ramp?
  • How long does it last: seconds, minutes, or hours?
  • Is the event planned, so capacity can be pre-warmed, or unexpected?
  • Which endpoints, keys, or tenants are affected?
  • Is the traffic read-heavy, write-heavy, or a mix?
  • Can requests be queued, delayed, or rejected, and what is the business cost of rejecting one versus serving it slowly?
  • Is the traffic from real users, or can bots and abuse be filtered?
  • Set a target: the system should degrade predictably rather than fail completely.

05Provision for the peak

You cannot autoscale your way out of an instantaneous spike. Capacity has to exist before the traffic arrives, either as headroom or as pre-warmed instances.

  • Size capacity for the peak you must survive, not the daily average.
  • Keep deliberate headroom so a surge or a lost node does not immediately saturate the system.
  • Scale the bottleneck tier, not every tier uniformly; the database or a downstream API is often the real limit.
  • Autoscaling helps with sustained growth but reacts after a delay and starts cold.
  • Use pre-warming before known events: add capacity, warm caches, open connection pools, and validate quotas.
  • Raise quotas and limits with third-party dependencies before the event, not during it.
  • Test the provisioned peak with a load test so the capacity number is measured rather than assumed.

Headroom versus autoscaling

  • Headroom is available immediately but costs money while idle.
  • Autoscaling is cheaper at baseline but cannot respond instantly to a step change.
  • The faster the ramp, the more the design depends on headroom and pre-warming.
  • Combine both: a baseline with headroom plus autoscaling for the sustained portion.

Why new instances are not ready instantly

  • A new instance must boot, load configuration, and establish connections.
  • Its caches are empty, so it sends more traffic to the database until they warm.
  • Health checks and load balancer registration add more delay.
  • A cold fleet can amplify a spike instead of absorbing it.

06Rate limiting and quotas

Rate limiting protects the system by capping demand before it reaches shared resources. It is the first line of defense when traffic cannot all be served.

  • Limit per user, per API key, per IP, or per tenant so one caller cannot consume the whole system.
  • Apply limits at the edge and again at critical internal boundaries.
  • Use a token bucket or leaky bucket to allow short bursts while enforcing a sustained rate.
  • Return a clear rejection, such as HTTP 429 with a retry hint, rather than letting the request time out.
  • Separate limits for reads and writes, and for expensive endpoints versus cheap ones.
  • Give priority or higher quotas to critical traffic such as payments or existing sessions.
  • Rate limiting also defends against bots and abuse that would otherwise look like a spike.

07Queueing and backpressure

A buffer converts a sudden burst into a backlog that can be drained later. It works only if the backlog is bounded and the system can eventually catch up.

  • Put a queue between the burst and the work that cannot run at that rate, such as writes or third-party calls.
  • Respond to the user with an accepted state when the work can genuinely be completed asynchronously.
  • Bound the queue; an unbounded buffer just delays the failure and consumes memory.
  • Apply backpressure when the buffer is full: slow producers, return retry-later, or reject.
  • Monitor queue depth, age of the oldest item, and consumer lag.
  • Make consumers idempotent because retries and redelivery are normal.
  • A queue absorbs a burst, but it cannot fix a sustained deficit between production and consumption.
Burst arrives→Enqueue work→Acknowledge quickly→Consumers drain at a safe rate→Backpressure if the queue fills

08Load shedding and admission control

When demand exceeds capacity, serving everything badly is worse than serving most requests well. Shedding is a deliberate choice about which requests to drop.

  • Reject requests early when the system is near saturation, before they consume threads and connections.
  • Shed low-value or expensive traffic first: bots, background jobs, analytics, and non-critical reads.
  • Protect critical flows such as checkout, login, and payments from the same limits as browsing.
  • Admission control decides at the front door whether a request may enter at all.
  • Use per-endpoint concurrency limits so one slow dependency cannot consume the whole pool.
  • Return a fast, honest error or a degraded response instead of a timeout.
  • A waiting room is admission control for a known event: hold users in a queue and let them in at a rate the system can serve.
  • Shedding must be observable so you can tell the difference between protection and an outage.

09Caching and origin offload

  • Serve repeated public responses from a CDN so they never reach the origin.
  • Use short TTLs and stale-while-revalidate so a surge does not force synchronous revalidation.
  • Cache at the edge, at the application, and in a shared cache, each absorbing a different share.
  • Precompute expensive responses before the event so the first request does not trigger the work.
  • Watch for cache stampedes: one popular key expiring under high traffic can hit the database hard.
  • Protect against cache penetration from requests for keys that do not exist with negative caching.
  • A CDN also absorbs bot traffic and retries that would otherwise reach your servers.

10Graceful degradation

Some features are essential and some are nice to have. Under extreme load, turn off the expensive optional work so the core flow survives.

  • Define a small set of critical user journeys and protect them explicitly.
  • Disable recommendations, personalization, live counters, and other expensive non-critical features under load.
  • Serve stale or approximate data when fresh computation is too expensive.
  • Switch to a simpler response format or a reduced page instead of failing.
  • Use feature flags so degradation can be enabled quickly without a deploy.
  • Make degradation automatic where possible, with a manual override for operators.
  • Tell the user when data is delayed or a feature is temporarily limited rather than silently lying.

11Protecting dependencies

  • Set aggressive timeouts so slow calls release resources instead of piling up.
  • Use circuit breakers to stop calling a failing dependency and let it recover.
  • Retry only idempotent operations, with exponential backoff and jitter to avoid synchronized retry storms.
  • Cap retries so a failure does not multiply load.
  • Isolate connection pools per dependency so one slow service cannot starve the rest.
  • Cache fallback responses for dependencies that are temporarily unavailable.
  • Apply bulkheads so a failure in one part of the system does not consume all shared capacity.

12Testing and operating for spikes

  • Run load tests at the expected peak and above it to find the real bottleneck.
  • Test the ramp shape, not just a steady high load; a step change is harder than a gradual climb.
  • Run game days and failure injection to see how degradation and shedding actually behave.
  • Measure p95 and p99 latency, error rate, saturation, queue depth, and autoscaling lag.
  • Watch the recovery tail: how long until latency and backlog return to normal after the spike.
  • Keep runbooks for enabling degradation, raising limits, and draining queues.
  • Review each real spike afterwards and adjust capacity, limits, and thresholds.

13Discussing traffic spikes in an interview

  • Ask whether the spike is planned, how large it is, how fast it ramps, and how long it lasts.
  • Ask what can be rejected or delayed, and which flows are business-critical.
  • Estimate peak load and compare it with current capacity and the bottleneck tier.
  • Describe the layers in order: edge caching and rate limiting, then admission control and queueing, then capacity.
  • Explain what degrades and what is shed when the system reaches its limit.
  • Cover retries, timeouts, and circuit breakers so one failure does not cascade.
  • Say what you would monitor and how you would validate the design before the event.

14Key takeaways

  • A spike is defined by magnitude, ramp speed, and duration; design for that shape rather than an average.
  • Keep headroom and pre-warm for known events; autoscaling alone reacts too late for a step change.
  • Rate limiting and admission control cap demand before it consumes shared resources.
  • Bounded queues absorb short bursts; backpressure prevents them from becoming an unbounded failure.
  • Shed low-value traffic and degrade non-critical features so the core flow survives.
  • Protect dependencies with timeouts, circuit breakers, capped retries, and jitter.
  • Test the peak, watch the recovery tail, and make degradation and shedding observable.

↔Trade-offs to say out loud

  • Headroom buys immediate absorption but costs money while idle; autoscaling is cheaper but reacts after a delay.
  • Pre-warming makes a known event safe but requires accurate forecasting and temporary extra capacity.
  • Rate limiting protects shared capacity but rejects or delays legitimate users during a genuine surge.
  • Queueing absorbs bursts and improves responsiveness but adds delay, duplicates, and a backlog that must drain.
  • Load shedding keeps the system responsive for most requests but explicitly fails some of them.
  • Graceful degradation preserves the core flow but gives users an incomplete or stale experience.
  • Aggressive timeouts free resources quickly but can abort requests that would have succeeded.
  • Circuit breakers protect the system from a failing dependency but can fail fast on traffic that might have worked.
  • Waiting rooms keep an event from collapsing but add friction and a second system to operate.
  • Autoscaling reduces idle cost but can oscillate, overshoot, and start cold instances that add load.

?Interview questions

Answer aloud before opening the outline.

1. What is a traffic spike?

A sudden, large increase in request rate above the normal baseline. Its impact depends on magnitude, ramp speed, duration, and which resources the traffic touches.

2. Why can a system with enough total capacity still fail during a spike?

Capacity is not instantly available where it is needed. Requests queue, latency rises, pools fill, retries add load, and autoscaling reacts late. The failure is about rate and concentration, not just total capacity.

3. How do you decide between headroom and autoscaling?

Use headroom for sharp, short, or unpredictable spikes where reaction time matters. Use autoscaling for gradual or sustained growth. Most systems combine a baseline with headroom and autoscaling for the rest.

4. Why isn't autoscaling enough for a ticket on-sale?

The spike arrives in seconds, while autoscaling takes time to detect load, launch instances, pass health checks, and warm caches. Pre-warming before the event and admission control during it are more reliable.

5. How does rate limiting help?

It caps demand before it reaches shared resources, keeps one caller from consuming the system, and gives a clear rejection instead of a slow timeout. Limits can be per user, key, tenant, or endpoint.

6. What is backpressure?

A signal that producers must slow down or retry later when the system cannot keep up. It is implemented with bounded queues, concurrency limits, or explicit rejection, and it prevents unbounded memory growth and collapse.

7. When does a queue help and when does it only hide the problem?

It helps a short burst that consumers can later drain. It hides the problem when consumers are persistently slower than producers, because the backlog grows without limit and the delay becomes unbounded.

8. What is load shedding and what would you shed first?

Deliberately rejecting or downgrading requests to protect capacity. Shed bots, background jobs, analytics, and expensive non-critical features first; protect checkout, login, and payments.

9. How do you prevent a retry storm?

Retry only idempotent operations, cap the number of attempts, use exponential backoff with jitter, and put a circuit breaker in front of failing dependencies. Respect server retry hints.

10. What is graceful degradation?

Serving a reduced but useful response when a feature or dependency is overloaded: disabling recommendations, returning stale data, or showing a simpler page instead of failing the request.

11. When would you use a waiting room?

For a known high-demand event where demand far exceeds capacity but users can wait, such as a ticket sale. It admits users at a controlled rate so the purchase path stays healthy, at the cost of friction.

12. What would you monitor during a spike?

Request rate, p95 and p99 latency, error and rejection rates, saturation of CPU, connections and pools, queue depth and age, cache hit ratio, consumer lag, autoscaling activity, and the time to recover after the peak.

13. How do you test for a spike before it happens?

Load test at and above the expected peak, with a realistic ramp shape and dependency latencies. Run game days that exercise degradation, shedding, and failover, and verify that recovery returns to baseline.

14. What is the biggest mistake in spike design?

Assuming autoscaling will handle it. Spikes are usually too fast and too concentrated for reactive scaling, so the design needs headroom, admission control, caching, and a plan for what to reject.

!Traps

  • Assuming autoscaling alone will absorb a sudden step change in traffic.
  • Provisioning for the daily average and calling it peak capacity.
  • Leaving queues unbounded and treating the growing backlog as success.
  • Retrying aggressively without backoff or jitter, turning a blip into an outage.
  • Scaling every tier except the actual bottleneck, such as the database or a third-party API.
  • Rate limiting critical payment or login traffic with the same policy as browsing.
  • Forgetting that new instances start with cold caches and empty connection pools.
  • Testing only steady high load and never the ramp or the recovery tail.

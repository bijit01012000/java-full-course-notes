01Why run in multiple regions

Many systems work well in a single region. Multi-region becomes justified when latency, availability, or regulation cannot be met any other way.

  • Latency: a user far from the region pays the speed-of-light round trip on every request, which hurts chatty protocols and page loads.
  • Availability: a regional outage takes down every availability zone at once; only another region survives it.
  • Disaster recovery: an RTO shorter than a rebuild-from-backup forces a warm standby somewhere else.
  • Data residency: laws or contracts may require data to stay inside a country or region.
  • Traffic locality: serving content and APIs near users can reduce egress cost and improve experience.
  • These benefits are not free: multi-region roughly multiplies infrastructure, operational, and testing complexity.

02The hard part: distance and data

  • Light in fiber travels roughly 200 km per millisecond, so a cross-continent round trip costs tens to hundreds of milliseconds before any processing.
  • Synchronous replication across an ocean makes every write wait for the far region, which raises write latency for everyone.
  • Asynchronous replication keeps writes fast but opens a window of lost or stale data during failover.
  • The speed of light cannot be engineered away, so the real design question is which operations may be stale and which may not.
  • Coordination services, distributed locks, and consensus across regions are slow for the same reason; keep them regional where possible.
  • Users notice inconsistency more than they notice a few milliseconds, so most global systems choose locality plus eventual consistency for reads.

03Topology options

  • Single region with backups: cheapest, but RTO is measured in hours and a regional outage is a full outage.
  • Active-passive (warm standby): a second region runs at reduced capacity and is promoted on failure; simple writes, slow recovery.
  • Active-passive (hot standby): the standby runs full capacity and replicates continuously; faster failover, double the cost.
  • Read-local, write-global: reads served nearby, writes routed to a primary region; good latency for read-heavy products.
  • Active-active: every region accepts writes; hardest to keep correct, best for latency and availability.
  • Cell or shard-per-region: each region owns a subset of users and writes only their data, which avoids most conflicts.
  • Pick the least complex topology that meets the latency and availability targets, and state what it cannot do.

04Routing users to a region

  • GeoDNS resolves a hostname to a nearby region; simple, but clients and resolvers cache the answer, slowing failover.
  • Anycast advertises the same IP from multiple regions and lets the network pick the closest; fast, but harder to control.
  • Latency-based routing sends users to the region with the best measured latency rather than strict geography.
  • Failover routing with health checks removes a failed region from DNS, subject to TTL and resolver behavior.
  • Keep TTLs short for failover records, and accept that some clients will hold stale answers anyway.
  • A global load balancer or API gateway can absorb routing logic and provide a stable endpoint while regions change.
  • Routing must also handle session affinity: a user's writes may need to reach the region that owns their data.

05Replicating data across regions

  • Use the database's native cross-region replication where possible; it handles log shipping, catch-up, and promotion.
  • Synchronous replication gives an RPO near zero at the cost of write latency and availability when the far region is unreachable.
  • Asynchronous replication keeps writes local and fast but allows recent writes to be lost on failover.
  • Semi-synchronous replication waits for one replica, usually in another AZ, and lets more distant copies lag.
  • Replicate the write-ahead log or change stream rather than dual-writing from the application, which can diverge.
  • Replication lag is a user-facing property: it decides how stale a read from another region can be.
  • Plan for replica catch-up after a partition; a region that was unreachable for an hour must not be promoted instantly.

06Consistency across regions

  • Strong consistency across regions requires coordination on every write or read, which is expensive at distance.
  • Eventual consistency lets each region serve local reads and converge later; users may see stale data briefly.
  • Read-your-writes can be preserved per user by routing their reads to the region that accepted their write, or by tracking a version.
  • Bounded staleness promises reads are no older than a defined window, which is often easier to reason about than pure eventual consistency.
  • Per-key consistency is usually enough: a user's own profile must be current, while a global counter can lag.
  • Be explicit about which screens tolerate staleness and which cannot, then design those paths separately.
  • A common pattern is to keep the authoritative write in one region and serve reads locally with a version check for critical views.

07Conflict resolution

  • Last-write-wins by timestamp is simple but silently loses updates and depends on synchronized clocks.
  • Version vectors detect concurrent edits and let the application decide instead of picking a winner blindly.
  • CRDTs converge automatically for counters, sets, and text, but not every business rule can be expressed as one.
  • Application merge — union of items, highest bid, latest confirmed status — often encodes the real business rule better than a generic algorithm.
  • Avoid concurrent writes to the same record by giving each key a home region; this is the simplest conflict resolution.
  • Record conflicts for review when automatic merging could produce a wrong business outcome, such as a double-booked seat.
  • Conflict handling must be tested with real partitions; a merge rule that only exists in a design doc is not a mechanism.

08Active-active writes

  • Partition data so each key is writable in exactly one region at a time; route the user to that region or proxy the write.
  • Use home region assignment by user ID so a user's writes stay ordered and conflict-free.
  • For genuinely multi-writer data, restrict operations to commutative ones: add to a set, increment a counter, append an event.
  • Accept that cross-region reads may be stale and disclose or hide that depending on the feature.
  • Keep request paths regional: a write that fans out to three regions multiplies latency and failure modes.
  • Failover of a home region requires transferring ownership, not just redirecting traffic, or writes will land in two places.
  • Active-active is a data design problem first and a traffic-routing problem second.

09Failover and failback

  • Detect regional failure from outside the region; a region's own monitoring cannot report its own outage.
  • Decide who is allowed to promote a region and require a quorum or an external coordinator to avoid two primaries.
  • Use fencing so a partitioned old primary cannot accept writes after a new one is promoted.
  • Redirect traffic with DNS, anycast withdrawal, or a global load balancer, and expect cached DNS to delay some clients.
  • Warm caches, connection pools, and autoscaling capacity in the standby before shifting full load.
  • Failback is a second migration: re-establish replication, reconcile divergent writes, and shift traffic gradually.
  • Every step should be a rehearsed runbook with a measured RTO, not an improvisation during an incident.
Detect region failure→Confirm quorum→Fence old primary→Promote standby→Shift routing→Warm capacity→Reconcile data→Fail back later

10Capacity planning for region loss

  • If traffic must survive losing a region, the remaining regions need enough headroom to absorb it, which means paying for idle capacity.
  • N+1 region capacity is the usual target for active-passive; active-active needs each region sized for its share plus failover spill.
  • Autoscaling helps only if the remaining region can scale fast enough; instance quotas and image pull times are real limits.
  • Shared dependencies — identity, config, DNS, third-party APIs — must also survive regional loss, or the extra region does not help.
  • Test at realistic peak, not average: failover usually happens during the busiest hour.
  • State the cost of the headroom explicitly; it is the price of the availability target.

11Data residency and compliance

  • Some data must remain within a jurisdiction, which constrains replication and backup locations.
  • Keep personally identifiable data in its home region and replicate only derived or aggregated data globally.
  • Encryption keys and audit logs may have their own residency rules separate from the primary data.
  • A global system may need region-aware routing so a user's data never leaves the allowed geography.
  • Deletion requests must propagate to every replica and backup, which complicates multi-region retention.
  • Compliance requirements are design constraints, not afterthoughts; they can rule out an otherwise elegant active-active design.

12Stateful supporting systems

  • Caches are usually regional; a global cache adds latency and a cross-region invalidation problem.
  • Queues and streams may replicate across regions, but consumers must handle duplicates after failover.
  • Search indexes are rebuilt or replicated per region; a stale index is a common post-failover bug.
  • Object storage should use cross-region replication for assets that must survive a regional loss.
  • Session stores and rate-limit counters need a regional home or a relaxed global approximation.
  • Each stateful dependency needs its own RPO and failover story; the application tier being multi-region is not enough.

13Operating and testing multi-region

  • Measure cross-region latency and replication lag continuously; both are leading indicators of user-visible problems.
  • Run failure drills that actually remove a region from routing, not just a single instance.
  • Verify that dashboards and alerts work when the primary region's observability stack is down.
  • Track which region served each request so debugging does not become guesswork.
  • Game-day the runbook with the people who will execute it at 3 a.m., including the failback path.
  • Keep configuration and schema changes backward compatible across regions, since rollouts cannot be atomic worldwide.
  • Record the achieved RTO and RPO from each drill and compare them with the target.

14Interview walkthrough and when not to go multi-region

  • Start with the requirement: which latency target, which availability target, which data residency rules.
  • Ask whether multi-AZ plus backups meets the target; if it does, do not add regions.
  • Choose a topology and say what it gives up: write latency, staleness, cost, or conflict complexity.
  • Explain routing, replication direction, and the consistency model per data type.
  • Describe failure detection, promotion, fencing, and the failback path.
  • Size the remaining capacity for region loss and name the shared dependencies that could still break.
  • Close with the tests and metrics that would prove the design works.
A single region with multi-AZ deployment and tested backups is often the correct answer for an interview unless global latency or regional survival is explicitly required.

↔Trade-offs to say out loud

  • Multi-region latency improves user experience near each region but adds cross-region replication and operational cost.
  • Active-active gives the fastest failover and best locality but requires conflict resolution for every writable dataset.
  • Active-passive keeps writes simple but leaves expensive idle capacity and a slower, riskier recovery.
  • Synchronous replication minimizes data loss but raises write latency and can block writes when a region is unreachable.
  • Asynchronous replication keeps writes fast but accepts an RPO greater than zero.
  • Global strong consistency simplifies reasoning but pays coordination latency on every request.
  • Home-region ownership avoids conflicts but creates a migration problem when that region fails.
  • Short DNS TTLs speed failover but increase query volume and still do not control client caching.
  • Extra regional headroom protects availability but is capacity that sits idle most of the time.

?Interview questions

Answer aloud before opening the outline.

1. Why would you run a system in multiple regions?

To reduce latency for distant users, survive a full regional outage, meet a short RTO, or satisfy data residency requirements. Each reason implies a different topology, so state the requirement before the design.

2. What is the hardest part of multi-region?

Keeping data correct across distance. Replication is either synchronous and slow or asynchronous and lossy, and concurrent writes in two regions need ownership or conflict resolution.

3. What is the difference between multi-AZ and multi-region?

Multi-AZ spreads instances across isolated data centers in one region and handles most hardware and facility failures. Multi-region survives the loss of an entire region but adds cross-region latency and data consistency problems.

4. Explain active-active versus active-passive.

Active-active serves traffic from multiple regions at once and needs conflict handling for writes. Active-passive keeps a standby that is promoted on failure, which is simpler but slower to recover and wastes standby capacity.

5. How do you route users to the right region?

Use GeoDNS, anycast, or a global load balancer with latency-based and failover routing. Keep TTLs short for failover records, and account for clients that cache DNS longer than you would like.

6. What are RPO and RTO?

RPO is how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time. RTO is how long recovery may take. They drive the replication mode and the topology, and both must be tested rather than assumed.

7. How do you keep data consistent across regions?

Choose per data type: strong consistency for money and inventory, read-your-writes for user profiles, eventual consistency for feeds and counters. Ownership by home region avoids conflicts for most user data.

8. How do you resolve write conflicts?

Avoid them with home-region ownership; otherwise use version vectors to detect concurrency and merge by business rule, or CRDTs for commutative data. Avoid naive last-write-wins for anything valuable.

9. How do you prevent split brain during failover?

Require quorum to elect a new primary, fence the old one with a token or lease, and make the write path reject a leader that cannot prove it holds the lease. Then reconcile any divergent writes.

10. Why is failback often harder than failover?

Both regions may have accepted writes, so data must be reconciled before replication resumes in the original direction. Caches, indexes, and queues also need rebuilding, and traffic must shift gradually.

11. How much spare capacity does a region need?

Enough to carry its own traffic plus the traffic of a failed region, at peak. That usually means N+1 regional capacity, and it must be tested with a real traffic shift.

12. How does multi-region affect deployments?

Rollouts cannot be atomic, so schema and API changes must be backward compatible across versions running in different regions. Deploy region by region and verify replication before moving on.

13. What would you monitor?

Per-region success rate and latency, cross-region replication lag, routing health, failover readiness, and capacity headroom for region loss. Also verify that observability works when one region is down.

14. When is a single region enough?

When multi-AZ redundancy and tested backups meet the availability target and users are not latency-sensitive across continents. Most systems do not need multi-region, and saying so is a strong answer.

!Traps

  • Adding a second region without deciding how writes behave when both regions are reachable.
  • Claiming active-active while quietly routing all writes to one region.
  • Using last-write-wins on valuable data and calling the conflicts resolved.
  • Forgetting that DNS caching makes failover slower than the TTL suggests.
  • Sizing the standby for normal load instead of the load it must carry during a failure.
  • Promoting a lagging replica without stating the data loss, or without fencing the old primary.
  • Ignoring shared global dependencies such as identity, config, and third-party APIs.
  • Never testing failover or failback, so the RTO and RPO are aspirational.

Dashboard
A curated set of charts and numbers designed to answer specific operational questions about a system, not a dump of every available metric.
Runbook
A written procedure that tells an on-call engineer how to diagnose, mitigate, and escalate a specific alert or failure mode.
Golden signals
Latency, traffic, errors, and saturation — the default top-level indicators of a service's health.
SLO
A service level objective: a target for a measurable reliability property, such as the fraction of requests served within a latency threshold.
Error budget
The amount of unreliability an SLO permits; burn rate tells you how fast it is being consumed.
Drill-down
A link from an aggregate view to a more detailed one, such as a panel to a trace, a log query, or a per-instance breakdown.
Escalation
The rule for moving an incident to another person or team when the first responder cannot resolve it within a defined time.
Playbook
A broader set of runbooks and decision guidance for a class of incidents, such as a dependency outage or a bad deploy.
Mitigation
Any action that reduces user impact — rollback, failover, feature flag off, traffic shed — even if it does not fix the root cause.
Postmortem
A blameless review after an incident that records the timeline, impact, contributing causes, and follow-up actions.

01The 3 a.m. problem

An alert says the payment service error rate is high. The dashboard has sixty panels, several look odd, and there are no notes on what to check first. The engineer who built the service is asleep in another time zone.

  • Without guidance, the responder starts guessing, and every guess costs minutes of user impact.
  • Dashboards are built during calm periods by people who know the system; they are read during incidents by people who do not.
  • The fix often takes two minutes — a rollback — while finding it takes twenty.
  • The gap between detection and mitigation is what dashboards and runbooks are designed to shrink.
  • Dashboards answer what is wrong and where; runbooks answer what to do about it.
  • Both are operational documents: they must be tested under realistic conditions, not just written once.

02What a dashboard is for

  • A dashboard exists to answer a small set of questions fast, not to display every metric the system emits.
  • Each panel should have a decision behind it: if this line is high, do X; if flat, do nothing.
  • The primary audience is someone under time pressure who did not build the system.
  • A good dashboard makes the abnormal obvious within seconds, before reading any labels.
  • It should also show when things changed — deploy markers, config changes, traffic shifts — because timing narrows causes.
  • Dashboards are not reports: long-term trend analysis belongs in separate, purpose-built views.
If you cannot state the question a panel answers, the panel is decoration and will be skipped during the incident.

03Layering dashboards

  • Fleet overview: one row of key services with health, error rate, and latency; the entry point during a broad incident.
  • Service dashboard: golden signals for one service, broken down by endpoint, status, and instance.
  • Dependency and resource view: databases, queues, caches, and pools that the service relies on.
  • Debug view: high-cardinality or per-instance detail used after the problem is localized, often closer to logs and traces.
  • Each layer should link down to the next, so the responder never has to search for the right dashboard mid-incident.
  • Layering prevents the classic mistake of putting every possible panel on one page.
  • Separate dashboards per audience — executives want SLOs, responders want saturation and error breakdowns.
Alert names the service and symptom→Fleet overview confirms scope: one service or many→Service dashboard locates the failing endpoint and instance→Dependency view checks the suspect downstream→Trace or log query confirms the mechanism

04Design rules that survive an incident

  • Put the most important signals in the top-left, where attention lands first.
  • Keep units and scales consistent across panels; mixing milliseconds and seconds causes misreads under stress.
  • Show rates and percentiles, not cumulative counters — a raw monotonically increasing total has no useful shape.
  • Use thresholds or shaded bands tied to SLOs so normal versus bad is visible without mental arithmetic.
  • Prefer few large panels over many small ones; a wall of sparklines is unreadable at 3 a.m.
  • Annotate panels with the deploy or config change markers that explain step changes.
  • Title panels with the question or the consequence, not just the metric name.

05Panels that actively mislead

  • Averaging latency hides the tail; a healthy average can coexist with a broken p99.
  • Clipping the y-axis makes a doubling look flat, and auto-scaling axes make small noise look dramatic.
  • Plotting multiple series with different units on one chart creates false correlations.
  • Gaps in data rendered as zero make an outage look like a drop to healthy zero traffic.
  • Aggregating across instances hides one bad replica behind nine healthy ones.
  • Long time ranges compress spikes into invisible blips; use a window matched to the alert.
  • Percentiles computed from averaged sub-percentiles are mathematically wrong and quietly reassuring.

06Wiring alerts, dashboards, and drill-downs together

  • Every alert should link directly to the dashboard that shows the affected service at the relevant time range.
  • Every dashboard should link to the trace search and log query for the same service, with filters prefilled.
  • Carry the alert's time window into the dashboard URL, so the responder lands on the incident window, not now.
  • Include the runbook link in the alert payload, so guidance is one click away on a phone.
  • Prefilled drill-downs remove the guesswork that consumes the first minutes of an incident.
  • Test the links: a dead runbook URL is worse than none, because it burns trust and time.

07Runbooks: purpose and anatomy

A runbook is a procedure for a specific, recognizable situation. It is written in advance so that nobody has to reason from first principles while users are affected.

  • Trigger: which alert or symptom this runbook covers, and what it looks like.
  • Impact: who is affected and how urgently, which decides whether to mitigate first or investigate first.
  • Diagnosis: the few checks that separate the likely causes, in order.
  • Mitigation: concrete actions with exact commands, flags, or console paths.
  • Verification: how to confirm the fix worked, using the same metric the alert used.
  • Escalation: who to contact, when, and what information to hand over.
  • A runbook should be short enough to follow on a phone at 3 a.m., with numbered steps rather than prose.

08Writing steps that work under stress

  • Use imperative numbered steps: check, run, expect, if not, go to step N.
  • Provide copy-pasteable commands with placeholders clearly marked, and say how to fill them.
  • State the expected output for each check, so the reader knows whether the result is normal.
  • Include decision points as explicit branches instead of relying on judgment.
  • Warn about actions that are irreversible or that lose diagnostic data, such as restarting before capturing heap dumps.
  • Note the blast radius of mitigations — failing over a region affects capacity elsewhere.
  • Keep a short if nothing works path that escalates rather than encouraging endless improvisation.

09Alert routing, ownership, and escalation

  • Every alert needs a named owning team; alerts with no owner become everyone's problem and nobody's task.
  • Route by service ownership rather than by infrastructure, so the responder knows the code.
  • Define escalation timers: if not acknowledged in N minutes, notify the secondary, then the team lead.
  • Separate pages (wake someone) from tickets (handle in business hours) and enforce the distinction.
  • Deduplicate and group related alerts so one root cause does not produce thirty pages.
  • Include the severity, the affected service, and the runbook link in the notification itself.
  • Review paging frequency per service; a noisy service is a reliability bug to fix, not a fact of life.

10The incident response loop

  • Acknowledge, then assess impact before investigating — the question is how bad, then why.
  • Mitigate first when users are affected: rollback, failover, disable the feature, shed load.
  • Communicate status on a known channel at a predictable cadence, even when there is no new information.
  • Record timestamps as you go; reconstructing a timeline from memory a day later loses accuracy.
  • Assign roles for larger incidents: an incident commander, a communicator, and subject-matter responders.
  • Preserve evidence before destructive actions — logs, traces, heap dumps, and the exact failing request.
  • Close with a blameless postmortem that produces owned, dated follow-up actions.
Alert fires and is acknowledged→Open the linked dashboard and confirm scope→Assess impact and decide mitigate versus investigate→Follow the runbook mitigation and verify→Communicate resolution→Write the postmortem and fix the detection gap

11Keeping documentation alive

  • Treat dashboards and runbooks as code: stored in version control, reviewed like changes, with owners.
  • Update the runbook during the incident, not weeks later when details are gone.
  • Delete panels nobody looks at; unused dashboards create false confidence and slow navigation.
  • Rehearse: game days and on-call shadowing expose steps that only work for the author.
  • Track whether runbooks were used and whether they helped; a runbook that was bypassed needs revision or retirement.
  • After each incident, ask what the responder had to figure out that the documentation should have told them.
  • Link runbooks from alerts, dashboards, and service catalogs, so they can be found without searching.

12Anti-patterns and failure modes

  • The wall of graphs: dozens of panels with no hierarchy, so the responder reads noise instead of signal.
  • The dashboard-only team: monitoring exists but no procedure, so detection is fast and response is slow.
  • The stale runbook: commands reference hosts, dashboards, or tools that no longer exist.
  • The hero dependency: the only person who understands the system is also the only one who can fix it.
  • The alert without a link: a page that names a symptom but provides no path to the cause or the fix.
  • The untested mitigation: a failover procedure that has never been executed and fails under pressure.
  • The silent outage: nobody is paged because the alert watches a cause metric that stayed green while users failed.

↔Trade-offs to say out loud

  • Comprehensive dashboards contain every signal but slow responders down through sheer volume.
  • Minimal dashboards are fast to read but may omit the detail needed for an unusual failure.
  • Detailed runbooks reduce dependence on experts but go stale quickly and can encourage blind following.
  • Short runbooks stay accurate but may lack the branches a real incident needs.
  • Aggressive paging catches problems early but causes alert fatigue and ignored pages.
  • High alert thresholds reduce noise but let user-visible degradation persist before anyone is woken.
  • Automated remediation shortens mitigation but can amplify a fault if the automation misdiagnoses.
  • Blameless postmortems surface honest causes but require organizational trust to be useful.

?Interview questions

Answer aloud before opening the outline.

1. What is a dashboard actually for?

Answering a small set of operational questions quickly, usually during an incident. It should make the abnormal obvious and point to the next step, not display every metric available. A panel with no decision behind it is noise that slows the responder down.

2. How many panels should an on-call dashboard have?

Few enough that the important signals are visible without scrolling or interpretation — typically one screen organized by hierarchy: health summary on top, golden signals below, then dependency detail. Anything beyond that belongs on a linked, deeper dashboard. The limit is attention, not screen space.

3. What should the top row of a service dashboard show?

Latency percentiles, error rate, throughput, and saturation for the service as a whole, plus the SLO status or error-budget burn if one exists. Those four answer is it broken and how badly. Everything else is a drill-down.

4. What is a runbook, and what does it contain?

A written procedure for a specific alert or failure mode: trigger, impact, ordered diagnostic checks, mitigation actions with exact commands, verification, and escalation. It is written in advance so the responder does not have to reason from scratch under pressure. It should be short, numbered, and tested.

5. How do dashboards and runbooks fit together?

The alert names the symptom and links to the dashboard, which shows scope and narrows the suspect; the dashboard links to the runbook, which gives the mitigation steps; the runbook ends by verifying on the same metric that alerted. Each artifact hands off to the next without searching.

6. Why do dashboards mislead during incidents?

Common causes are averaged latency hiding the tail, auto-scaling axes exaggerating noise, gaps drawn as zero, and aggregation across instances hiding one bad replica. Another is a time range that does not match the alert window, so the responder sees a healthy chart for the wrong period.

7. Mitigate first or diagnose first?

If users are affected and a safe mitigation exists — rollback, failover, disable a feature — mitigate first and diagnose afterwards with the evidence preserved. Diagnose first only when the mitigation is risky, irreversible, or would destroy the data needed to prevent recurrence.

8. How do you decide alert severity and routing?

Base severity on user impact and urgency, not on which metric fired. Page only for conditions that need immediate human action, route to the owning team, and define escalation timers with a secondary and a lead. Everything else becomes a ticket or a dashboard signal.

9. How do you keep runbooks from going stale?

Store them in version control with owners, update them during the incident rather than after, and rehearse them in game days. Link them from alerts so broken links are noticed immediately. Retire procedures nobody uses instead of letting them rot.

10. What makes a runbook hard to follow at 3 a.m.?

Prose instead of numbered steps, missing expected outputs, unexplained commands, and branches that depend on expert judgment. The reader is sleepy, possibly on a phone, and under pressure. Explicit run this, expect this, if not go here structure is what makes it usable.

11. What is the role of a postmortem?

It reconstructs the timeline, quantifies impact, identifies contributing causes, and produces owned follow-up actions. Done blamelessly, it surfaces the process and detection gaps that allowed the incident. The measure of success is whether the same failure can recur.

12. How do you know your dashboards are good?

Watch someone who did not build them respond to a simulated alert. If they can determine scope and the next step within a minute or two, the dashboard works. If they scroll, squint, or ask which panel matters, it needs fewer panels and clearer hierarchy.

!Traps

  • Building a dashboard with every available metric and calling it observability.
  • Alerting without linking a runbook, then expecting the responder to rediscover the procedure at 3 a.m.
  • Writing runbooks as narrative documentation rather than executable, numbered steps.
  • Letting graphs auto-scale or clip axes so the severity of a change is misjudged.
  • Paging on cause metrics like CPU while user-facing error rate is not alerted at all.
  • Leaving dashboards and runbooks unowned, so they silently rot after the author leaves the team.
  • Skipping postmortem follow-ups, guaranteeing the same incident has the same twenty-minute detection gap.
